Inside Bangladesh’s
political family
advantage.

A familiar surname can help someone enter politics. A study spanning 1973–2026 asks a more revealing question: what happens after they get in?

By Nahid Jahan Bhuiyan

Winning a seat in parliament and getting a place in the cabinet are two different achievements. The first puts a politician inside the legislature. The second puts them closer to the machinery of government.

That distinction changes how we look at political dynasties. Instead of asking only how many MPs have relatives in politics, we can ask where those connections are most concentrated.

The answer in this research is striking: family-linked politicians account for 22.4% of parliamentary seat records, but 40.8% of records coded for cabinet service during the same parliamentary term. Their share rises to 50.3% among records coded for full-minister or prime-minister service in that term.

These are patterns in a historical dataset, not proof that a family connection caused any particular appointment. But they point to a story that is much bigger than a list of famous surnames.

First, what are we counting?

The research follows 170 political families and identifies 401 family-linked MPs. Its “dynastic” classification includes family founders as well as later generations, using the research’s recorded kinship links. It does not mean every person inherited a seat from a parent.

The main dataset has 4,527 seat records, not 4,527 different people. It includes repeat terms, by-elections and simultaneous constituency wins. There are 2,700 named individuals and 13 vacancy placeholders. This distinction matters whenever a chart counts seats rather than people.

Where the gap grows

The cabinet is the revealing part.

A parliament can look relatively open while its executive positions are much more concentrated. In the research dataset, the family-linked share grows as we move from all seats to cabinet service and then to senior executive service.

The comparison below lets you look at this in two ways. “During that term” counts executive service matched to the parliamentary term. “Over the career” counts records belonging to someone who achieved the relevant rank at some point in their career. Those measures answer different questions.

The gap does not tell us whether family-linked politicians are more competent, better connected, more trusted by party leaders, or some combination of these. The paper’s explanation focuses on gatekeeping: the people who distribute nominations and portfolios may value an established family’s networks and loyalty.

That is a proposed explanation for the pattern. Measuring who holds office is easier than establishing why a leader selected them.

The first-term test

What if experience isn’t the whole explanation?

One obvious objection is that established political families have been around longer. Perhaps their MPs simply have more experience.

Looking at first-term records gives us a more focused comparison. In the replication dataset, 86 of 453 family-linked first-term records are coded for full-minister or PM service during the term. Among non-dynastic first-term records, the count is 96 of 2,055.

Both groups are new to parliamentary service. Their routes to senior executive office still look very different. This suggests that the family pattern cannot be understood only by counting terms already served.

It does not rule out other forms of experience before parliament, differences in party position, or the selection of particular candidates. A first-term MP need not be a newcomer to politics.

The long view

Power can shrink. A family’s share can grow.

It is tempting to imagine dynastic influence rising smoothly over time. The record is much less tidy. In the dataset, family-linked seat share falls to 11.5% in the fourth parliament, rises to 30.7% in the seventh, and stands at 15.4% in the thirteenth.

There is another, less obvious pattern inside individual parties. A party can lose many seats and emerge with a larger proportion of family-linked MPs among those who remain.

The Awami League’s eighth-parliament records illustrate it: 33 of 64 are classified as dynastic—51.6%. The BNP’s seventh-parliament records show 37 of 120—30.8%. The paper calls this a post-defeat “bunker” pattern.

The arithmetic is important. A rising percentage need not mean families won more seats. It can mean other politicians lost more of theirs. A smaller surviving group can be more family-heavy than the party was before defeat.

Families do not always fit inside one party.

The family registry also records 60 of 170 families under more than one party label. That broad definition includes Independent labels and can reflect changes over time; it does not establish that relatives simultaneously operated in rival camps.

Use a narrower definition—families appearing in at least two of the Awami League, BNP and Jatiya Party—and the count is 44. The difference is a useful reminder: a dramatic claim can depend on a quiet coding choice.

The paper interprets cross-party ties as a possible form of political insurance. That is an interesting hypothesis, not evidence that every such family coordinated a survival strategy.

    Who comes next

    The families remain. The generation changes.

    A dynasty is not a fixed cast of characters. One of the clearest changes in the data is the balance between family founders and their successors.

    In the first parliament, 5 of 54 dynastic seat records belong to second- or third-generation heirs: 9.3%. By the tenth parliament in 2014, heirs account for 58 of 102—56.9%, overtaking founders. In the thirteenth, the count is 44 of 54—81.5%.

    That last number needs its denominator: 81.5% of dynastic records, not 81.5% of parliament. The overall family-linked share and the share of heirs within that group can move in different directions. Both are needed to understand the handover.

    A shared family connection does not mean a shared route to power.

    The paper’s analysis of women makes that point especially clearly. It distinguishes wives and widows from daughters and granddaughters, then compares their later executive careers.

    The reported gap is substantial: 9.3% of the wives/widows group attained cabinet office, compared with 35.5% of the daughters/granddaughters group. Women who served in both reserved and general seats had a reported 53.8% career Cabinet rate, although that group contains just 13 people.

    These comparisons raise a question about opportunity: can a family connection open the door to parliament while leaving further advancement restricted? They do not show that one pathway causes better careers, or that reserved seats themselves create the difference. The groups can differ in many other ways.

    How much can the data tell us?

    A strong pattern is still not a controlled experiment.

    The working paper goes beyond counting. Its statistical models compare dynastic and non-dynastic records while accounting for factors such as party, parliament, seat type and parliamentary experience. It also examines first-term records and different definitions of executive service.

    The forest plot below is a compact view of its reported model estimates. Each dot is an estimated difference; each horizontal line shows a 95% confidence interval. “Percentage points” measure an absolute gap: going from 10% to 27% is a rise of 17 percentage points, not 17%.

    Accounting for recorded differences does not account for every difference. Wealth, prior political work, local organization, personal networks and decisions about whose relatives count can still matter. A family founder may be classified as dynastic because a relative enters politics later; that label is not necessarily a connection inherited before their own first election.

    The dataset is also a reconstruction. This article checks arithmetic against the accompanying files; it does not independently establish every genealogical link, ministerial date or historical source. The 2026 observations are part of that research snapshot, not a live parliamentary tracker.

    The most defensible takeaway is therefore a focused one: in this research, family-linked representation is more concentrated among executive-service records than among parliamentary seat records overall. Establishing the mechanisms behind that gap requires more evidence.

    The question to take away

    Who gets elected is only
    part of the story.

    To understand access to power, look at who gets promoted after the election—and how the opportunities of one generation become the starting points of the next.

    The updated manuscript, figures and interactive graphics use 934 strictly in-term Cabinet-coded records (381 dynastic), 597 strictly in-term full-minister-coded records (300 dynastic), and 2,508 first-term seat records from the replication CSV. The manuscript also reports alternative counts for a narrower governing-coalition definition; those answer a different question. Its 2,713 total entries include 13 vacancy placeholders, leaving 2,700 named people. The figures and interactive charts label their own denominators so these measures can be read on their terms.

    Percentages describe coded records unless a person-level group is explicitly named. They are not counts of distinct cabinet offices or a claim about any individual’s motives or merit.
